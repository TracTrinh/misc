The writing instrument

More than a pen.

The panhinda does not lay ink on the leaf. Its steel tip cuts a fine groove, while the bronze handle carries a visual map of Buddhist teaching. The instrument makes the act of writing inseparable from what is being preserved.

The first meeting

A journey, a red cloth, and a photograph.

The friendship behind this website began with an order. Before they had met in person, the friend who later created this site commissioned several Ola leaf books from Sahampathi, then travelled to Sri Lanka to collect them and meet their maker for the first time.

The completed books were photographed on a red cloth given to him by a monk at Na Uyana. Sahampathi later shared that this photograph introduced his work to a wider Buddhist community, leading to new connections with meditation monasteries in China and Indonesia, as well as practitioners in many other places.

That single meeting quickly grew into a bond like brothers. This website is one way that friendship supports Sahampathi’s work: by helping more people discover his books, and by supporting his effort to keep a rare handmade tradition alive.

Trust across generations

A book offered with reverence.

This family photograph shows Sahampathi’s father offering an Ola leaf book to Most Venerable Nauyane Ariyadhamma Mahāthēra.

It preserves more than a ceremonial moment. The family’s work has earned trust among meditation monasteries and sacred places, with their books finding homes at monasteries, meditation centres and stupas where the teachings are practised and honoured.

That trust was built before Sahampathi took up the work himself. Today, every new commission continues a relationship between the family workshop and Buddhist communities across generations.

More stories live around these books—in Sahampathi’s workshop, in the people who commission them, and in the communities that receive them. This collection will grow as those stories are gathered.
